# Structure Guide: Story and Web-Reading Diagnostics

Two structural lenses for this stage: story structure (for flagship or narrative-driven pages) and web-reading behavior (for every page). The editor diagnoses against both; it never rewrites the section to fit.

## When Story Structure Applies

Only major messaging -- a company story, a flagship page, an about page -- warrants full narrative treatment. Do not demand a five-phase story from a routine product or spec page. Where a story exists, its job is to give every derivative asset a shared, aligned picture of the customer's situation.

## Story Phases and Per-Phase Failure Modes

Verify all five phases are present, in order:

1. **The shift** -- a change in the customer's world the reader recognizes as real. Failure modes: too big or too vague (generic "the world has changed" openings); a shift the reader feels no pressure from; a shift set in the wrong arena for this reader.
2. **The villain** -- the core problem, given a distinct, nameable face (an object, an approach, a concept). Failure modes: unnamed or abstract villain; a villain too large to plausibly tackle; a villain the reader has not already recognized as a problem; a villain that casts a group inside the customer's own company as the enemy -- always flag this as divisive.
3. **Three obstacles** -- concrete subproblems the villain creates, each mapping one-to-one to a benefit claimed elsewhere on the page. Run the mapping explicitly: an obstacle with no matching benefit, or a benefit with no matching obstacle, is a structural break.
4. **The dream** -- how the customer wishes things were, in two parts: the tangible outcome and how it will feel. A dream with no emotional half is half-built -- flag the missing part.
5. **The solution** -- the product enters last, at high altitude, connecting the value proposition and benefits back to the villain and obstacles, ending on the dream. Flag any dive into feature or mechanism detail here -- it breaks the story's register.

## Paragraph Caps

The finished story runs four paragraphs. Check each against its cap:
1. The shift and its direct effect on the customer -- maximum three sentences, minimal preamble.
2. The villain plus the three obstacles -- maximum five sentences.
3. The dream, with its emotional half -- maximum three sentences.
4. The value proposition and benefits, closing on why it matters -- no feature detail.

Is the perspective (second person by default) consistent across all four paragraphs, or does it drift between second and third person mid-story? Flag mixed perspective.

## Post-Draft Story Questions

- Are the villain and the three obstacles problems the customer has already identified themselves, or problems framed only from the seller's internal analysis?
- Could the villain, as written, offend or divide any part of the intended audience?
- Is the shift too big, too vague, or too clichéd? The usual fix direction is starting the story later, closer to what the customer already notices.
- Does the solution paragraph stay out of the technical weeds through to the end?

## Web-Reading Diagnostics (Every Page)

**Scanning.** Is the copy skimmable -- grouped into modules, headed, bulleted where appropriate? Flag three-plus consecutive paragraphs with no subheading. Does each module stand alone, or does it depend on prose far above it? Is secondary information linked out rather than inlined?

**Front-loading.** Does the copy open on the main idea, or build to it through warm-up context? In any bad-news, urgent, or compliance-adjacent message, is front-loading mandatory -- no throat-clearing, no unnecessary alarm language, no jokes before the point?

**Headings.** Do headings, read alone in sequence, form a usable outline? Does heading capitalization stay consistent one way across the piece?

**Sentence-level.** Are there long words where short ones would do? Sentences carrying more than one idea? Passive constructions with no deliberate purpose? Flag over-cutting (strings of clipped sentences reading stiff) as readily as bloat.

## Per-Format Notes

**Landing and marketing pages.** Does the page answer the reader's real questions (what it is, who it is for, what it does, why it matters, how it works, where to get it) in plain language? Flag any attempt to explain everything on one page instead of pointing onward. Do headlines use active verbs tied to what the reader should do next? Is comparative puffery ("better," "best") flagged wherever it appears unconcretized?

**Vertical or industry pages.** Does the page's structure translate technical capability into the vertical's actual stakes before naming the mechanism, or does it lead with acronyms the reader must decode first?

**Technical / spec pages for a carrier or wholesale reader.** Does the structure lead with the technical specification the reader is scanning for, since here the mechanism-first ordering is expected rather than a failure?

**Full-page pass.** Read the assembled page in reading order. Does it flow, avoid keyword and capitalization inconsistency in headings, and sound like it was written by one voice rather than stitched from multiple drafts?
