# V-B-F Method: Diagnosing Claim Architecture

The primary structural diagnostic for any claim-bearing copy. Every line of messaging is one of three things. Classify before judging. The editor diagnoses against this hierarchy; it never rewrites the line to fit it.

## Classification Definitions

- **Feature** -- something the product has or can do. The sentence describes the product.
- **Benefit** -- something new the customer can do, be, or feel because of a feature. The sentence describes a customer-side state change.
- **Value proposition** -- the big win multiple benefits add up to. A larger goal the customer is trying to achieve, usually tied to a business-wide outcome.

## Miscategorization Check

- Is a claimed "benefit" actually about the product, not the customer? If the subject and emphasis is the product, it is a feature in disguise. Flag it.
- Is a claimed "value proposition" really just one benefit with no aggregation and no larger goal attached? Flag as under-leveled.

## Ordering Check

- Read only the headline and first line. Does value lead, benefit come second, feature come last? If a feature or a product name leads instead, flag as **inside-out ordering** -- it forces the reader to work out what the mechanism means for them instead of leading with what they can now do. Skimmers only read headlines; a feature-led headline forfeits the whole message.

## Structure Check

- Is there more than one value proposition competing at the top level of this page or section? Flag as unfocused.
- Are there four or more benefit pillars? Flag -- this exceeds what a reader can recall and usually signals unresolved internal disagreement about what matters most.
- Are there fewer than three benefits? Check coverage (below) before flagging -- this may be complete, or may be missing a pillar.
- Are extra component types (value drivers, elevator pitches, capability lists, problem statements) piled in alongside the core hierarchy? Flag as clutter that buries what matters.

## Cohesion Check

Four questions, run in order:

1. **Ordering** -- is the most important message highest? A benefit more critical than the value proposition sitting above it is a defect.
2. **Vagueness** -- could many companies in this category claim this same value proposition? The more crowded the category, the more specific the top line must be.
3. **Coverage** -- do the benefits together give a full picture of what is possible? Name any differentiating capability that has no home in the hierarchy.
4. **Distinctness** -- are the benefits genuinely different from each other, or do two of them share near-identical feature lists underneath? Flag the overlap as a duplicate.

## Nesting Check

- Where the same one-value-proposition/three-benefit unit repeats at multiple levels (company page, product page, feature page), is each level a complete, self-contained unit? Does the value proposition stay visible at each level, or does it drown in supplementary material the deeper the page goes?

## Multi-Benefit Swirl

- Does a single sentence or section carry more than one benefit payload mashed together? Flag it -- the reader cannot say what the product actually does. This usually comes from fear of leaving something out; displaced claims can be relocated to a lower tier or a different asset, not lost.

## The So What Trace

The tool for locating any claim on the feature-to-value ladder and testing whether it sits at the right rung.

**Upward trace.** Take the claim as written and ask: so what does that get the reader? Write down each rung until no further reason can be produced.
- Does the claim need three or more rungs before reaching anything the reader cares about? Flag as **sitting too low** -- feature-level copy.
- Is the claim already at the top of the chain, generic enough that any competitor's product could make it? Flag as **sitting too high** -- the real differentiation lives further down.

**Downward trace.** Does every value or benefit claim trace down to a real, present capability? A claim with no feature underneath it is unsubstantiated -- flag it.

**Future-capability gate.** Is this claim only redeemable by a roadmap item or unreleased feature? Flag as overpromising -- query whether a present-tense version exists.

**Honesty check.** Trace as a reader who knows nothing about the product would. Insider shortcuts and "obvious" rung-skips are invisible to that reader -- if the trace only works with inside knowledge, the claim fails.

## Jargon Hunt, Translate, Legitimacy

**The hunt.** Jargon is produced unconsciously -- scan actively for four flavors:
1. Corporate-speak (leverage, synergy, bandwidth as a metaphor, agility).
2. Buzzwords and hype (transform, revolutionize, supercharge, reimagine).
3. Internal lingo -- terms that mean nothing outside the company.
4. Deep technical talk that loses a buying-committee reader who is not the engineer.

**The translation demand.** For each flagged term: what does this actually mean through the buyer's eyes? If it cannot be restated as a concrete, picturable outcome, flag it as saying nothing. If it can, flag it as **untranslated jargon** and state what the phrase apparently means -- without writing the replacement.

**The legitimacy test.** A term the target reader actually uses in their own work is not jargon -- it builds credibility. Two questions must both pass for the defined reader:
1. Will it be universally understood by this specific audience?
2. Is it meaningful -- does it point at one specific referent, or will different readers in this audience interpret it differently?

A term that passes (1) but fails (2) is still jargon for this audience.

**Coined terms.** Default to clarity over coining. Does an invented category name or internal term drive the story and land with readers, or does it require explanation that the copy never gives? If it confuses, flag it and note that the plain, outcome-describing version is what the reader actually needs.
