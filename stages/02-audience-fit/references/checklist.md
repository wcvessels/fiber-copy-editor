# Audience-Fit Checklist

The editor diagnoses audience mismatch. It never rewrites the line to fix it -- it names who the copy is failing and hands the problem back.

## Who Is This For

- Who is the primary reader on this page, by role and buying stage?
- What buying job is that reader trying to do right now?
- Who signs for what this page sells -- and does the copy speak to that person, or only to the technical evaluator who influences but does not sign?
- Is the opening written for the person evaluating the service, the person approving the budget, or both -- and if both, does one get the majority of the message with the other confined to a single slot, rather than the message diluted evenly across both?
- Does this page know which specific buyer segment it is for, or is it addressed to generic "businesses"?

## Altitude and Register

- Does the first screen make the reader's risk or gain clear before it names products, acronyms, or transport types?
- If the reader is nontechnical, can they explain the business reason for the service after one read?
- If the reader is technical (carrier, wholesale, network architect), does the page preserve enough domain-specific vocabulary to read as credible, or has it been stripped down to generic reassurance?
- Has any consumer or residential register ("stay connected," "speed of light," "keep you connected") crept onto a page selling a dedicated, contracted, enterprise-grade service? Name this failure: **consumer-register-on-B2B**.
- Is the altitude too high -- a vague outcome any provider could claim, disconnected from what the page actually delivers?
- Is the altitude too low -- transport-layer mechanism talk with no consequence attached, losing the budget holder even if it satisfies the engineer?

## Jargon Budget

- If the reader is a carrier or wholesale buyer, is the technical vocabulary (route miles, lit/unlit, diverse pathways, wavelength, Layer 1) precise enough to signal a serious counterparty, or has it been vagued down in the name of "simplicity"?
- If the reader is nontechnical, is every acronym and transport term (DIA, SASE, MPLS, CJIS, MEF) translated into risk, cost, or outcome on first use -- or does the copy assume the reader already knows what it means?
- Does the page's jargon density match the audience profile set at intake, or does it read as written for a different reader than the one the page's own framing implies?

## Champion vs. Signer

- Is the reader targeted actually the champion (the person researching and pushing the deal), or has the copy been pitched at the signer's altitude when the signer never reads this page?
- In a mixed buying committee, is the shared need carrying most of the message, with the divergent need (technical detail vs. budget/risk framing) confined to a defined slot rather than spread evenly and diluting both?
- If this page funnels multiple audiences to one CTA, does the CTA still feel safe for the least technical -- or least senior -- stakeholder in that committee?

## Vertical and Compliance Context

- Does the page correctly identify which regulated vertical (healthcare, finance, government, education) it is written for, or is it generic across verticals that actually have different fears?
- For a public-sector or regulated-industry reader, does the tone stay calm and confidence-building, or does any joke, pun, or cute metaphor risk trivializing a stakes-heavy purchase decision? Name this failure: **tone-undercuts-stakes**.
- Does the page connect infrastructure or technical capability to the vertical's actual fear (breach exposure, audit failure, continuity of service, budget defensibility) rather than assuming the acronym alone carries the weight?

## Cross-Check

- If this same page were shown to a reader from the wrong audience segment (e.g., a carrier buyer shown budget-holder copy, or a budget holder shown a wholesale spec sheet), would they bounce immediately, or does the page work reasonably for both? A page that fails this cross-check either needs a segment split or has not chosen its reader.
