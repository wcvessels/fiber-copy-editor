# Sentence Diagnostics

Line-level vocabulary for the LOC (Lower-Order Concerns) batch note. Every finding names the on-page feature, the predicted reader effect, and a named failure the writer can look up -- never a rewrite. Feelings ("unclear," "clunky," "flat") are not findings; trace every impression to a feature below before writing the note. Run this pass on a near-final draft, after the HOC gates (03-07) have settled structure -- never on material that may still be restructured.

## 1. Clarity

Clear sentences put characters in the subject slot and their actions in the verb slot.

- **Subject scan.** Underline the first seven or eight words (skip a short opener). Flag if the simple subject is an abstraction, or no verb has landed by word eight. **ABSTRACT SUBJECT** -- readers have no character to build the sentence around.
- **Buried action.** The real action sits in a noun bolted to an empty verb (be, have, make, provide, occur). Spot it by suffix (-tion, -ment, -ance, -ness), gerunds-as-nouns, or a noun identical to its verb. **BURIED ACTION** -- the verb tells the reader nothing; content migrated into a nearby noun. Worst case: the action-noun is the subject.
- **Keep-list.** Don't flag an action-noun that compresses the prior sentence (flow work), replaces an awkward "the fact that," names the verb's object, or is familiar enough to function as a character. Flagging these is an editor error, not a writer error.
- **Deleted doer.** Flag "there is/was" plus an action-noun, or any buried action with no recoverable doer. **DELETED DOER** -- readers guess who acts, and in ambiguous copy they guess wrong.
- **Voice check.** "Feels passive" is often an abstract-subject problem, not a grammar problem -- diagnose buried actions first. For a true passive, ask: does the reader need the agent named? does it let the sentence open familiar and close new? does it keep one character in the subject slot? A yes to any is a keep. **UNEARNED PASSIVE** -- fails all three.
- **Noun pileup.** Flag invented strings of three or more nouns stacked as modifiers. Established compounds and field terms are exempt. **NOUN PILEUP** -- the reader must reverse-engineer the grammar before the meaning.
- **Agency audit.** Where a claim carries risk, cost, or a promise, list the subject of every clause. **AGENCY DODGE** -- the responsible party (the company, the offer's terms) never occupies a subject slot; abstractions do the acting while the reader is left to infer who's accountable. High-priority check for claims and offer copy.

## 2. Flow

Cohesion is pairwise (sentence to sentence); coherence is cumulative (does the passage add up). A passage can pass every seam and still fail the whole -- run both.

- **Seam test.** At each sentence boundary, the last words of one sentence should set up the first words of the next. **BROKEN SEAM** -- each sentence opens cold.
- **Old-before-new.** Open with what the reader already knows; close with what they don't. **NEWS UP FRONT** -- the sentence opens on the unfamiliar with nothing to hang it on. This is invisible from the writer's own seat -- everything they wrote feels familiar to them -- so run it mechanically.
- **Topic-string test.** Underline the first seven or eight words of every sentence in a passage. They should form a small set of related ideas naming the passage's real subject. **TOPIC HOPPING** -- the openings don't reduce to a coherent set, even if each sentence is individually clear.
- **Throat-clearing.** Count words before the topic arrives. **THROAT-CLEARING** -- stacked transitions and hedges delay the point past what the reader will tolerate.
- **Jigsaw check.** If every seam holds but the passage still drifts, check for unrelated subjects across sentences or no sentence stating what the passage is about. **JIGSAW WITHOUT A PICTURE** -- the pieces connect; no picture emerges.

## 3. Emphasis

The end of a sentence is its stress position -- what lands there reads as the point.

- **Tap test.** Read aloud, stress the last three or four words. **FLAT ENDING** -- the stressed words don't deserve the emphasis.
- **Anticlimax tail.** Trailing qualifiers, attributions, or restatements after the real claim. **ANTICLIMAX TAIL** -- the reader's last impression is the least important thing in the sentence. Direction: trim the tail, or shift the new information rightward.
- **Term placement.** A new term or product concept opening the sentence lands on a reader with nothing to attach it to. **TERM DUMPED EARLY** -- direction: move the term to the end, after context.
- **Framing stress.** The opening sentence of a section should end on the concept the section develops. **BURIED THEME** -- the organizing idea surfaces mid-passage instead of up front, so the reader assembles the wrong frame first.

## 4. Concision

Concision is a reader judgment -- just the words the claim needs -- not a word-count target. Run after clarity; a sentence can have a clean subject and verb and still be wordy.

- **FILLER TIC** -- deletable with zero meaning change (actually, basically, various, really, generally). Test: delete it, check nothing changed.
- **REDUNDANT PAIR** -- fossil synonym pairs (full and complete, each and every, hope and trust). Keep one.
- **INFERABLE PADDING** -- a modifier the noun already implies (terrible tragedy, future plans, past history) or a category word a specific term already implies (period of time, pink in color).
- **PHRASE BLOAT** -- a stock phrase compressible to one word (despite the fact that / even though; prior to / before; there is a need for / must).
- **NEGATIVE ALGEBRA** -- an explicit negative plus an implicitly negative word (preclude, fail, lack, unless) forces the reader to compute. More than one negation in a clause is the flag.
- **DECORATIVE MODIFIER** -- delete every adverb and pre-noun adjective, then restore only what the reader needs; what stays struck was decoration.
- **METADISCOURSE BURY** -- words about the writing (it has been observed that; this section discusses) outweighing words about the subject.
- **OVER-HEDGE / BULLDOZER** -- qualifiers stacked past the point of information (over-hedge), or intensifiers doing the work evidence should do -- clearly, obviously, undoubtedly (bulldozer). Calibrate against the evidence actually on offer, not a word count.

## 5. Shape (sprawl)

Length is never the finding by itself; a controlled long sentence outreads a choppy split. Every sprawl note names which cause applies.

- **SLOW START** -- a long introductory element (over roughly seven or eight words) holds the main clause hostage in the reader's memory.
- **OVERLOADED SUBJECT** -- the whole subject runs past seven or eight words, usually with a nominalization or a long relative clause riding it.
- **WEDGED INTERRUPTION** -- more than a couple of words stuffed between subject and verb, or verb and object.
- **BACKWARD SENTENCE** -- the sentence's actual claim arrives after its own support instead of before it, so the reader can't judge relevance until it's over.
- **CLAUSE CHAIN** -- relative clauses hung on relative clauses (which... which... that...) after the verb. Direction menu: split into sentences, or promote a trailing clause with a fresh noun that relaunches it.
- **LOPSIDED PAIR / FALSE AND** -- coordinated elements that don't match grammatically, or a bare "and" hiding a real cause/contrast relation the writer hasn't named.
- **LOST BRANCH** -- in a long coordination, the reader hesitates over where the second branch attaches, or which noun a pronoun reaches back to.
- **TWO-WAY MODIFIER / DANGLER** -- a modifier with more than one plausible host, or an opening modifier whose implied subject doesn't match the main clause's actual subject.
- **HEAVY-FIRST SERIES** -- a coordinate series that runs long-to-short instead of short-to-long, so it stops abruptly.

## 6. Plain language, bias-free, and accessibility

Applies to every public-facing piece; strictness scales with how action-critical the copy is.

- **Audience-first organization.** Is the piece organized around the reader's top task, not the company's internal structure? Most important information first; parallel construction in headings and lists.
- **Verbs over nominalizations; active voice** -- except where the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or the passive manages emphasis (see Clarity above). Flag agent-free evasions ("it has been determined") as accountability gaps, not just style.
- **One term per concept.** Flag synonym-swapping (renaming the same offer or feature mid-piece implies the referent changed) and flag numbing over-repetition toward the other extreme.
- **Jargon.** Fine for a technical/procurement audience; for a broader reader, flag for a plain-language gloss.
- **Bias-free language.** Diagnose the implied assumption -- who the wording excludes or defaults to -- and supply the observation, not a lecture. Relevance test: any personal-characteristic reference must serve the point. Symmetry test: attributes stated for everyone or no one. Audit patterns across the whole piece (all-male examples, one group always in a subordinate role), not sentence by sentence.
- **Accessibility.** Alt text present for every image and chart; structure encoded by real headings, not bold paragraphs; descriptive link text (never "click here"); no meaning carried by color alone; legibility judged at final display size.

## Note-writing protocol

One finding per note: the feature, the predicted reader effect, the failure name in caps, and a revision direction the writer executes -- never rewritten text. When a passage trips multiple checks, lead with the highest-leverage layer: flow, then clarity, then shape, then emphasis, then concision. When a writer defends a flagged construction, the standard is the exemption lists and a stateable reason -- never taste against taste.
