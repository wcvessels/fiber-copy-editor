# Edit Letter Format

The edit letter is this stage's primary deliverable. It assembles findings from the upstream gates (02 audience-fit, 03 claims, 04 proof, 05 differentiation, 06 structure, 07 CTA) into one document the writer can act on, plus a separately attached LOC batch note for sentence-level findings. The editor diagnoses; the letter never contains replacement copy. Draft the letter only after every upstream pass is complete -- writing it early produces a letter the later passes will contradict.

## Sequencing

The order is load-bearing.

1. **Open with a good-faith read.** One or two genuine sentences establishing that the piece was read open-mindedly and that its intent was understood. No boilerplate.
2. **What's working.** Name genuine strengths before any problem appears, specific enough to be useless as flattery -- the exact move that worked and why. This is structural, not softening: it is also the floor the writer needs before the problems land as coaching instead of verdict (see `note-tags.md`).
3. **Problem summary.** One paragraph naming the core issue(s) driving most of the findings, in a few precise phrases. Do not itemize here. Cap the named issues at the HOC cap (set in `shared/concern-definitions.md`) -- these are the Higher-Order Concerns (HOC); everything else routes to the LOC batch or gets logged and dropped.
4. **Path forward, paired with each problem.** For every named HOC, state the feasible direction and the scale of the fix: a line-level flag, a targeted rewrite prompt the writer executes, or an escalation (see below). Never leave a hard finding without a next step in the same breath.
5. **Itemized HOC findings.** Each finding gets a location, the evidence, the mechanism (what it does to the reader), and the target effect -- see `query-conventions.md` for query/flag form and `note-tags.md` for tagging. Group findings by the gate that produced them (claims / proof / differentiation / structure / CTA) so the writer can see which lens is talking.
6. **LOC batch pointer.** Reference the attached sentence-level batch note (built from `sentence-diagnostics.md`) as its own artifact. Do not fold line-level findings into the HOC list -- mixing scales buries the structural issues under sentence noise.
7. **Revision logistics.** State what is required before the next round versus what is open (writer's call), and what happens next. See the Required/Open convention in `note-tags.md`.
8. **Close.** Reiterate that the piece can work, and invite the writer's own read on the plan before revision starts.

## How problems are named and evidenced

- **Locate everything.** Abstract findings get dismissed; located findings get fixed. Every citation points to a specific passage.
- **Name once, tag recurrences.** Give each recurring failure a short label (the note-tag or diagnostic name from `sentence-diagnostics.md`) at first instance; later instances get the tag only.
- **Argue reader effect, not writer failing.** "This buries the proof point a reader needs before the CTA," never "you always bury the lede."
- **Be exhaustive where the problem is exhaustible.** If a claim-specificity failure appears six times, cite six locations, not a sample -- partial lists let the writer discount severity.
- **No silent changes, ever.** Every flag carries its rationale. Nothing the writer cannot see and evaluate belongs in the letter.

## The HOC-cap discipline

Change capacity is finite. A letter with more required structural issues than the cap (`shared/concern-definitions.md`) fails regardless of how correct each one is -- the writer cannot absorb it and nothing gets fixed.

- Log every finding from every gate. Deliver only the highest-leverage ones, up to the cap, as HOC items.
- Choose by reader impact and gate severity: an audience-fit gate failure or a structural (06) finding outranks a diction-level claims issue.
- What doesn't make the cap does not vanish. State plainly that the full findings set exists and was triaged, and route what's cuttable to the LOC batch or to a noted backlog for the next round.
- Never silently drop a finding to hit the cap without saying so.

## Developmental-verdict letters (escalation)

Some pieces arrive with a problem this diagnose-only pipeline cannot fix inside its normal budget. That is not a failure of the gates -- it is what the gates are for.

**Escalation triggers** (any one is sufficient):
- The 02 audience-fit gate fails outright: wrong reader, wrong register, wrong funnel stage, and the fix is not a line edit.
- The 06 structure pass surfaces a piece organized around a topic instead of a claim, or a stakes-proof-offer-CTA order that cannot be reached by reordering existing material.
- Claims contradict each other across the piece, or the differentiation pass finds no ownable position at all.
- The defects found are the kind that will actually get escalated on -- reputational or deal-risk exposure, not just polish.

**What an escalation letter contains, differently:**
- The core dilemma named in one paragraph, same as any HOC problem summary, but framed explicitly as a structural or strategic problem, not a diagnose-only fix.
- A feasibility statement: is a revision achievable within the existing material, or does this need to route back to brief/intake as a different piece.
- No itemized line-level findings past what's needed to evidence the escalation. Do not do structural work inside a feedback-package pass.
- An explicit routing statement: this needs a scope decision before any further diagnostic round runs on this piece.

**Scope discipline.** Never smuggle a structural fix into a normal-scope letter. When a pass surfaces a problem bigger than its lens, name it, evidence it, and stop -- escalate it as a scope decision, not through unilateral expansion of the pass.

## Collaboration framing so the writer acts

- **Editor owns the diagnosis; writer owns the prose.** Every HOC and LOC finding is a flag or a query, never a rewrite.
- **Offer candidate directions, not single verdicts,** where more than one fix is defensible. State a recommendation and the reasoning for it; let the writer choose.
- **Frame around strengths, not suppression.** Pitch the revision as organizing and amplifying what already works, not as removing what's broken.
- **Congratulate first, balance the ledger.** The first message acknowledges the work; problems follow separately, and never arrive with zero acknowledgment of what's working.
- **Track everything.** Keep the findings log (source for the HOC/LOC triage above) as the evidence that nothing raised was silently dropped.
