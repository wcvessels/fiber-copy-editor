# Positioning Method: Diagnosing Copy Against Declared Position

The editor diagnoses against the declared positioning document, never against personal opinion of what the positioning should be. Every finding traces to one of the six components below or to a named drift pattern.

## Positioning Components as Copy-Audit Checks

**Competitive alternatives.** What would the target buyer actually do if this offering did not exist? Not the vendor's competitor list -- the buyer's real fallback (stay on the incumbent's legacy service, bond existing connections, keep the current contract, do nothing until renewal forces the issue). Does the copy position against what the buyer would really do otherwise, or against a rival the buyer has never evaluated?

**Unique attributes.** Capabilities the alternatives lack -- provable facts, not sentiments. Does every differentiation claim on the page trace to a declared, provable attribute? Is "superior service" or "easy to manage" backed by a specific fact (support ratios, installation intervals, certifications, audited uptime), or is it a value dressed as an attribute?

**Value and proof.** The chain is feature, then benefit, then value -- what it has, what that enables, what that means for the buyer's goal. Is there a value claim on this page with no attribute behind it (unsupported benefit)? Is there an attribute with no value stated in front of it (spec-sheet copy that makes the buyer do the so-what translation themselves)?

**Target market characteristics.** Not broad firmographics -- the trait that predicts a buyer will care intensely (multi-site operations, uptime-sensitive revenue, a compliance regime, an in-house IT gap). Does the copy speak to that specific who-cares-a-lot segment, or to everyone? Copy addressed to everybody connects with nobody.

**Market category.** The frame of reference the copy claims membership in. What category does the page actually imply -- through vocabulary, comparisons, and feature emphasis, not just the label used -- and does it match the declared category?

**Relevant trends (optional).** A trend explains why the offering matters right now; it is not a substitute for a category. Is a trend layered on top of a clearly declared category, or standing in for one?

## Frame Diagnostics

Central question: does the category and competitive alternative this copy implies match the declared position?

1. Read the page as a stranger. From headline, subhead, and comparisons alone, what category and what alternative does it imply?
2. Compare against the declared position. Does the label match but the implied assumptions diverge?
3. Check for drift: has the offering outgrown the frame the copy still uses (**product drift**)? Has the buyer's self-conception moved while the copy stayed still (**market drift**)?

Named symptoms of a wrong frame -- flag any that appear:
- Does the copy spend its opening effort un-explaining its own category label? Constant differentiation-by-denial signals a frame forcing bad comparisons.
- Does the category label set an expectation the offering does not meet, so the reader hunts for a missing baseline feature?
- Does the real differentiator read as an anomaly rather than a strength in this frame?
- Does the implied comparison invite a fight the offering cannot win on the category's own terms?
- Is the differentiator presented with no mapped audience, inviting a cheaper-weaker-version reading?
- Does existing-customer language about the offering diverge from the page's own language? Trust the customer framing as evidence.

## Competitive-Alternative Test

For every superiority claim (faster, more reliable, simpler, better supported, more scalable):

1. List the true alternatives for this segment, ranked by how commonly buyers actually choose them -- typically: stay with the incumbent, bond or stack an existing connection, delegate to a third party, do nothing.
2. Which alternative is this claim implicitly measured against?
3. Is the yardstick wrong? An easier-than claim only holds against the buyer's actual comparison point, not an obscure rival the buyer has never evaluated.
4. Does the copy argue only against named rivals when the dominant real alternative is the status quo -- or only against doing nothing when the declared strategy is displacement of a named incumbent? Either mismatch is a flag.
5. Is the claimed differentiation genuinely absent from the alternative, stated as a provable fact, and mapped to something this segment cares about?

## Category and Trend Checks

- **Borrowed-category mismatch**: does the page claim a category whose baseline features or price expectations it does not meet?
- **Unwinnable-category framing**: does the copy invite a head-to-head comparison against the category's entrenched leader on the leader's own terms, when the actual strategy is a subsegment play?
- **Subsegment claim without a real unmet need**: is a vertical or niche framing decorative, with no distinct unmet need behind it?
- **Trend-as-category**: does the copy substitute a trend (automation, digital transformation) for an actual category, leaving the reader unable to evaluate what is being offered?
- **Unlinked trend**: is a trend invoked with no demonstrable connection to the offering's actual attributes? Flag as credibility-damaging decoration.

Absence of any trend is not a defect -- do not flag copy for failing to invoke one.

## Positioning-to-Copy Traceability

**Forward trace.** Is the declared category legible from the page without insider knowledge? Does each declared value theme appear on the page, proportional to its priority -- the lead theme leading? Do consideration attributes (what buyers weigh when deciding) dominate, with retention attributes (support experience) in a proof role rather than leading?

**Reverse trace.** Does every claim on the page map to a declared positioning component? A claim with no home is a drift candidate -- either invented by the copy or missing from a stale positioning document. Does the page's vocabulary match the declared vocabulary, or carry legacy terms from an earlier position?

**Drift severity, increasing:**
1. Emphasis drift -- claims all trace, but proportions are wrong.
2. Vocabulary drift -- terminology diverges across pages.
3. Claim drift -- value claims appear with no declared attribute behind them.
4. Yardstick drift -- comparisons shift to alternatives the declared position does not name.
5. Frame drift -- the page's implied category no longer matches the declared one. Report at the positioning level, not the copy level.

If findings of this kind cluster across multiple pages, flag the likely cause: campaign copy derived from other campaign copy rather than from one master source, compounding small departures into position-level drift.
